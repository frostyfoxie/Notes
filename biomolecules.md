Fehling’s solution and Tollens’ reagent are mild alkaline oxidizing agents used to identify reducing sugars among carbohydrates. A positive reaction occurs when a carbohydrate contains a free aldehyde or ketone group, oxidizing the sugar into a carboxylic acid salt while reducing the metal ions in the reagent. \[1\] (https://www.chemistrysteps.com/oxidation-of-monosaccharide-carbohydrates/), \[2\] (https://www.google.com/search?q=product&prds=pvt:hg,pvo:29,productid:14076567589685043790&ibp=oshop), \[3\] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bO97PzFYZgI)

Composition of the Reagents

• Fehling’s Solution. Prepared immediately before use by mixing equal volumes of two separate liquids. Fehling’s A is an aqueous blue solution of copper(II) sulfate, and Fehling’s B is a clear alkaline solution of potassium sodium tartrate (Rochelle salt) in sodium hydroxide. \[1\] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zApxgVy9f3U), \[2\] (https://mlsu.ac.in/econtents/1842_fehlings.pdf), \[3\] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bO97PzFYZgI)  
• Tollens’ Reagent. Commonly called ammoniacal silver nitrate, it is prepared by adding aqueous ammonia to silver nitrate until the initial silver oxide precipitate dissolves. This forms the active diamminesilver(I) complex, \\(\[\\text{Ag}(\\text{NH}\_3)\_2\]^+\\). \[1\] (https://www.chemistrysteps.com/oxidation-of-monosaccharide-carbohydrates/)

Reaction Mechanism with Carbohydrates

• Aldose Oxidation. In an aqueous solution, cyclic monosaccharides exist in equilibrium with their open-chain forms. The free aldehyde group (\\(-\\text{CHO}\\)) of an aldose is easily oxidized to a carboxylate ion (\\(-\\text{COO}^{-}\\)) by both reagents. \[1\] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bO97PzFYZgI), \[2\] (https://www.chemistrysteps.com/oxidation-of-monosaccharide-carbohydrates/)  
• Ketose Isomerization. Although ketones generally do not react with mild oxidants, ketose sugars like fructose give a positive result. The alkaline medium causes ketoses to undergo keto-enol tautomerism, converting them into aldoses that can then be oxidized. \[1\] (https://byjus.com/jee/fehlings-solution/), \[2\] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zApxgVy9f3U), \[3\] (https://www.chemistrysteps.com/oxidation-of-monosaccharide-carbohydrates/)  
• Fehling’s Reduction. Copper(II) ions (\\(\\text{Cu}^{2+}\\)) in the deep blue tartrate complex are reduced to copper(I) ions (\\(\\text{Cu}^{+}\\)) upon heating. This forms an insoluble brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide (\\(\\text{Cu}\_2\\text{O}\\)). \[1\] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MFq4Typf4_8&t=53), \[2\] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zApxgVy9f3U), \[3\] (https://www.scribd.com/document/417217133/Barfoed-s-Tollen-s-Fehlings)  
• Tollens’ Reduction. Diamminesilver(I) ions (\\(\\text{Ag}^{+}\\)) are reduced to elemental metallic silver (\\(\\text{Ag}\\)). On a clean glass test tube, this deposits as a characteristic shiny silver mirror. \[1\] (https://www.scribd.com/document/417217133/Barfoed-s-Tollen-s-Fehlings), \[2\] (https://www.chemistrysteps.com/oxidation-of-monosaccharide-carbohydrates/)

An aldehyde group (\\(-\\text{CHO}\\)) and an alpha-hydroxy carbonyl group are called reducing because they easily donate electrons to mild oxidizing agents, converting themselves into carboxylic acids while reducing metal ions like \\(\\text{Ag}^{+}\\) or \\(\\text{Cu}^{2+}\\). A hemiacetal is a functional group where a single carbon atom is bonded to both an alcohol group (\\(-\\text{OH}\\)) and an ether group (\\(-\\text{OR}\\)), which allows sugar rings to open and react.

Why \\(-\\text{CHO}\\) is a Reducing Group

• Weak Carbon-Hydrogen Bond. An aldehyde carbon is bonded to a hydrogen atom (\\(-\\text{CHO}\\)). Because oxygen is highly electronegative, it pulls electron density away from this carbon, making the \\(\\text{C}-\\text{H}\\) bond very easy to break.  
• Easy Oxidation. Mild oxidants like Tollens' reagent (\\(\\text{Ag}^{+}\\)) or Fehling's solution (\\(\\text{Cu}^{2+}\\)) can easily insert an oxygen atom into that weak \\(\\text{C}-\\text{H}\\) bond. The aldehyde loses electrons (oxidizes) to form a stable carboxylate ion (\\(-\\text{COO}^{-}\\)), which forces the metal ions to gain electrons (reduce).

Why Alpha-Hydroxy Carbonyls are Reducing

• Base-Catalyzed Rearrangement. Ordinary ketones lack a hydrogen atom on the carbonyl carbon, so they resist mild oxidation. However, an alpha-hydroxy ketone has an \\(-\\text{OH}\\) group attached to the carbon right next to the \\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\) group.  
• Enediol Intermediate. Both Fehling's and Tollens' reagents are alkaline (basic). In a basic solution, the alpha-hydroxy ketone rearranges through a flat intermediate called an enediol (via keto-enol tautomerism), shifting the double bond and turning the ketone into an aldehyde (\\(-\\text{CHO}\\)) that can then be oxidized.

What is a Hemiacetal?

• Structure. A hemiacetal forms when an alcohol (\\(-\\text{OH}\\)) reacts with an aldehyde (\\(-\\text{CHO}\\)). The resulting central carbon atom ends up attached to four different groups: an \\(-\\text{OH}\\) group, an \\(-\\text{OR}\\) (ether) group, an \\(-\\text{R}\\) (carbon) group, and a \\(-\\text{H}\\) atom.  
• Ring Formation in Sugars. In carbohydrates like glucose, the \\(-\\text{OH}\\) group on carbon-5 loops around and attacks the \\(-\\text{CHO}\\) group on carbon-1\. This creates a stable cyclic ring, and carbon-1 becomes a hemiacetal carbon (also called the anomeric carbon).  
• Reversibility. Hemiacetals are unstable in water and exist in a dynamic equilibrium with their open-chain forms. Because the ring constantly opens and closes in solution, the free \\(-\\text{CHO}\\) group is temporarily exposed, allowing the sugar to act as a reducing agent.  
• Hemiacetal vs. Acetal. If a hemiacetal reacts with a second alcohol molecule, the \\(-\\text{OH}\\) group is replaced by another \\(-\\text{OR}\\) group, forming a locked acetal (a glycosidic bond). Acetals cannot open back up in basic solutions, which is why sugars like sucrose are non-reducing.

1\. D and L Configuration (Pages 10 & 11)

When chemists draw carbohydrates in a flat straight-line diagram (called a Fischer projection), they put the most oxidized group (like \\(-\\text{CHO}\\) or \\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\)) at the top.  
• How to find D or L. Look at the chiral carbon (asymmetric carbon) farthest from the top carbonyl group. For a 6-carbon sugar like glucose, this is carbon-5 (the second carbon from the bottom).  
• D-form. If the \\(-\\text{OH}\\) group on this bottom-most chiral carbon points to the right, it belongs to the D-family.  
• L-form. If the \\(-\\text{OH}\\) group on this bottom-most chiral carbon points to the left, it belongs to the L-family.  
• The Big Trap (Page 11). To turn a D-sugar into its L-sugar (like turning D-Galactose into L-Galactose), you cannot just flip the bottom \\(-\\text{OH}\\) group to the left. An L-sugar is the complete mirror image (enantiomer) of the D-sugar, meaning you must flip every single chiral carbon from right to left and left to right.  
• No Link to Optical Rotation. Capital D and L only tell you the 3D arrangement of atoms relative to glyceraldehyde. They do not tell you whether the sugar rotates plane-polarized light clockwise (+ / d) or counterclockwise (- / l). For example, D-Fructose is actually levorotatory (-92°).

Diastereomers are stereoisomers (molecules with the exact same molecular formula and atom connections, but different 3D arrangements) that are not mirror images of each other. \[1\]

Breaking Down the Two Types of Stereoisomers

To understand diastereomers easily, compare them with their opposite, enantiomers:  
• Enantiomers (Exact Mirror Images). Think of your left hand and right hand. They look identical, yet they are exact mirror images that cannot be stacked perfectly on top of each other. In sugars, D-Glucose and L-Glucose are enantiomers because every single chiral carbon is flipped. \[1\]  
• Diastereomers (Non-Mirror Images). Think of two siblings who look similar, share the same parents, and have the same height and weight, but are clearly not mirror reflections of each other. In sugars, D-Glucose and D-Galactose have the exact same formula (\\(\\text{C}\_6\\text{H}\_{12}\\text{O}\_6\\)) and atom connections, but because only one carbon (C-4) is flipped while the other carbons stay the same, holding a mirror up to D-Glucose will never give you D-Galactose. \[1\]

How Epimers Fit In

• Special Category. Every epimer is automatically a diastereomer.  
• The Difference. Diastereomers can differ at one, two, or more chiral carbons (as long as they don't all flip to become a mirror image). An epimer is simply a strict, specific type of diastereomer that differs at only one chiral carbon. \[1\]

1\. Basic Facts About Glucose (Pages 16 & 17)

• Monosaccharide. It is the simplest sugar unit and cannot be broken down (hydrolyzed) into smaller sugars.  
• Formula & Structure. It is an aldohexose (\\(\\text{C}\_6\\text{H}\_{12}\\text{O}\_6\\)) with 4 chiral centers (C-2, C-3, C-4, and C-5).  
• Stereoisomers. Using the formula \\(2^{n}\\) (where \\(n = 4\\) chiral centers), an aldohexose has \\(2^4 = \\mathbf{16}\\) possible stereoisomers.  
• Optical Rotation. Natural D-glucose is dextrorotatory (d or +), meaning it rotates plane-polarized light to the right. \[1\]

2\. Chemical Reactions of Glucose (Pages 18 & 19)

Chemists use specific reactions to prove the structure of glucose piece by piece: \[1\]  
• Proving a Straight 6-Carbon Chain (\\(\\text{HI} / \\text{Red P}, \\Delta\\)). Prolonged heating with hydriodic acid reduces all oxygen groups completely, forming n-hexane. This proves all 6 carbons are linked in a straight line without branching. \[1\]  
• Proving a Carbonyl Group (\\(\\text{NH}\_2-\\text{OH}\\) or \\(\\text{HCN}\\)). Glucose reacts with hydroxylamine (\\(\\text{NH}\_2\\text{OH}\\)) to form an oxime (and with \\(\\text{HCN}\\) to form a cyanohydrin). This proves there is a \\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\) group present. \[1\]  
• Proving 5 Alcohol Groups (\\(\\text{Ac}\_2\\text{O} / \\text{Pyridine}\\)). Reacting glucose with excess acetic anhydride converts all five \\(-\\text{OH}\\) groups into acetate groups (\\(-\\text{OAc}\\)), forming glucose pentaacetate. Because this compound is stable, the 5 \\(-\\text{OH}\\) groups must be attached to 5 different carbons. \[1\]  
• Proving the Carbonyl is an Aldehyde (\\(\\text{Br}\_2/\\text{H}\_2\\text{O}\\) or Tollens'). Mild oxidation with bromine water oxidizes only the top \\(-\\text{CHO}\\) group into a carboxylic acid (\\(-\\text{COOH}\\)), forming D-gluconic acid. (Note: Fructose does not react with \\(\\text{Br}\_2/\\text{H}\_2\\text{O}\\), so bromine water is used to distinguish glucose from fructose!) \[1\]  
• Proving One Primary Alcohol (\\(\\text{HNO}\_{3}\\)). Nitric acid is a strong oxidizing agent. It oxidizes both the top aldehyde (\\(-\\text{CHO}\\)) and the bottom primary alcohol (\\(-\\text{CH}\_2\\text{OH}\\)) into carboxylic acids, yielding a dicarboxylic acid called D-glucaric acid (or saccharic acid). \[1\]  
• Reduction to Sugar Alcohol (\\(\\text{NaBH}\_{4}\\) or \\(\\text{H}\_2/\\text{Ni}\\)). Mild reduction turns the top \\(-\\text{CHO}\\) group of glucose into \\(-\\text{CH}\_2\\text{OH}\\), forming D-sorbitol. (Interestingly, reducing fructose gives a mixture of both D-sorbitol and D-mannitol because the \\(sp^{2}\\) ketone carbon at C-2 can be attacked from either side.) \[1\]

3\. Osazone Formation (Pages 20, 21 & 22)

• What happens. When glucose is heated with 3 equivalents of phenylhydrazine (\\(\\text{Ph}-\\text{NH}-\\text{NH}\_2\\)), it forms a yellow crystalline solid called an osazone. \[1\]  
• How the 3 molecules are used.  
1\. The first molecule reacts with C-1 (\\(-\\text{CHO}\\)) to form a hydrazone.  
2\. The second molecule oxidizes the C-2 alcohol (\\(-\\text{CHOH}-\\)) into a ketone (\\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\)), releasing aniline (\\(\\text{PhNH}\_{2}\\)) and ammonia (\\(\\text{NH}\_{3}\\)) as byproducts.  
3\. The third molecule reacts with the newly formed ketone at C-2 to complete the osazone. \[1\]  
• Why Glucose, Mannose, and Fructose Form the Same Osazone. The reaction destroys the chiral center at C-2 (turning it into a flat \\(\\text{C}=\\text{N}\\) double bond) while leaving C-3, C-4, and C-5 untouched. Because D-Glucose, D-Mannose (its C-2 epimer), and D-Fructose (which has a \\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\) at C-2) all have the exact same arrangement on C-3, C-4, and C-5, they all produce the exact same osazone. \[1\]

Reaction 6: Mild Reduction with \\(\\text{NaBH}\_{4}\\) or \\(\\text{H}\_2 / \\text{Ni}\\)

• The Product from Glucose. D-Sorbitol (also called glucitol). Notice that C-1 is now \\(-\\text{CH}\_2\\text{OH}\\), which is not a chiral center (because it has two identical hydrogen atoms attached to it). Since C-1 is already flat and symmetric in the product, reducing D-glucose gives only one single product: D-Sorbitol.  
• The Important JEE Comparison at the Top of Page 18 (Fructose Reduction). Look at the circled structure at the top of Page 18—that is D-Fructose. In fructose, the \\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\) group is at C-2, which is a flat \\(sp^{2}\\)-hybridized carbon. When \\(\\text{H}\_2 / \\text{Ni}\\) or \\(\\text{NaBH}\_{4}\\) reduces that flat \\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\) group into a tetrahedral \\(sp^{3}\\) \\(-\\text{CH}(\\text{OH})-\\) group, C-2 becomes a brand-new chiral center!  
• Why Fructose Gives Two Products. Because the C-2 carbonyl carbon is completely flat, the hydride (\\(\\text{H}^{-}\\)) can attack equally well from either the left face or the right face:  
• If the new \\(-\\text{OH}\\) ends up on the right, you get D-Sorbitol (identical to reduced glucose).  
• If the new \\(-\\text{OH}\\) ends up on the left, you get D-Mannitol (identical to reduced D-mannose).  
• Therefore, mild reduction of D-Fructose always gives an epimeric mixture of D-Sorbitol and D-Mannitol.  
Lock this sixth piece in:  
• D-Glucose + \\(\\text{NaBH}\_{4}\\) or \\(\\text{H}\_2/\\text{Ni}\\) \\(\\rightarrow \\) Only D-Sorbitol (because C-1 becomes \\(-\\text{CH}\_2\\text{OH}\\), which is achiral).  
• D-Fructose + \\(\\text{NaBH}\_{4}\\) or \\(\\text{H}\_2/\\text{Ni}\\) \\(\\rightarrow \\) D-Sorbitol + D-Mannitol (because C-2 becomes a new chiral center with \\(-\\text{OH}\\) on either the right or the left).  
Does the difference between reducing glucose (1 product) and fructose (2 products) make complete sense, and are you ready to dive into Reaction 7: Osazone Formation (Pages 20–22)?<br><br>

Let's move on to Reaction 7: Osazone Formation (Pages 20, 21, and 22). This is one of the most heavily tested reactions in JEE Organic Chemistry, so let's break down both how it happens step-by-step and why it is so useful.

Reaction 7: Osazone Formation with Excess Phenylhydrazine

• The Reagent. 3 equivalents (excess) of phenylhydrazine (\\(\\text{Ph}-\\text{NH}-\\text{NH}\_2\\)) with heat.  
• The Overall Result. When D-glucose is heated with 3 molecules of phenylhydrazine, both C-1 and C-2 react to form carbon-nitrogen double bonds (\\(\\text{C}=\\text{N}-\\text{NH}-\\text{Ph}\\)), producing a bright yellow, insoluble crystalline solid called glucosazone (an osazone).

Step-by-Step Mechanism (Why 3 Molecules Are Needed — Page 21)

Normally, an aldehyde only reacts with 1 molecule of phenylhydrazine. However, because glucose has an \\(-\\text{OH}\\) group right next door at C-2 (making it an \\(\\alpha \\)-hydroxy aldehyde), the reaction goes two steps further:  
• Step 1 (1st Molecule of \\(\\text{PhNHNH}\_{2}\\)): Hydrazone Formation. The first molecule of phenylhydrazine attacks the C-1 aldehyde (\\(-\\text{CHO}\\)), removing water (\\(\\text{H}\_2\\text{O}\\)) to form glucose phenylhydrazone (\\(-\\text{CH}=\\text{N}-\\text{NHPh}\\)). (If the reaction is kept cold, it stops right here.)  
• Step 2 (2nd Molecule of \\(\\text{PhNHNH}\_{2}\\)): Oxidation of C-2\. Upon heating, the hydrazone tautomerizes, and a second molecule of phenylhydrazine acts as an oxidizing agent. It oxidizes the secondary alcohol (\\(-\\text{CHOH}-\\)) at C-2 into a ketone (\\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\)) while the C-1 group becomes an imine (\\(-\\text{CH}=\\text{NH}\\)). In this step, the second phenylhydrazine molecule gets reduced and breaks apart into aniline (\\(\\text{PhNH}\_{2}\\)) and ammonia (\\(\\text{NH}\_{3}\\)).  
• Step 3 (3rd Molecule of \\(\\text{PhNHNH}\_{2}\\)): Final Osazone. Now there are two \\(\\text{C}=\\text{N}\\) / \\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\) double bonds at C-1 and C-2\. A third molecule of phenylhydrazine condenses with the newly formed C-2 ketone (releasing \\(\\text{H}\_2\\text{O}\\)), while the C-1 imine exchanges with phenylhydrazine (releasing \\(\\text{NH}\_{3}\\)), locking in the final osazone structure.  
• Why Does It Stop at C-2? You might wonder why the reaction doesn't keep marching down the chain to oxidize C-3, C-4, and C-5\. Once C-1 and C-2 both have \\(=\\text{N}-\\text{NHPh}\\) groups attached, the molecule folds into a super-stable 6-membered ring stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonding (chelation), which completely stops any further reaction at C-3\.

The Famous JEE Concept on Page 22 (Same Osazone!)

• Loss of C-2 Chirality. Look closely at the final osazone structure on Page 21\. In D-glucose, C-2 was a chiral \\(sp^{3}\\) carbon. In the osazone, C-2 is now part of a flat \\(sp^{2}\\) double bond (\\(\\text{C}=\\text{N}-\\text{NHPh}\\)), which means its chiral center is completely destroyed! Only carbons C-3, C-4, and C-5 keep their original 3D stereochemistry.  
• Why Glucose, Mannose, and Fructose Form the Exact Same Osazone:  
1\. D-Glucose and D-Mannose are C-2 epimers—they differ only at C-2 and have identical C-3, C-4, and C-5\. Since C-2 chirality is destroyed during osazone formation, both yield the exact same osazone.  
2\. D-Fructose has a \\(\\text{C}=\\text{O}\\) at C-2 and \\(-\\text{CH}\_2\\text{OH}\\) at C-1, while its C-3, C-4, and C-5 are identical to D-glucose. When fructose reacts with 3 equivalents of phenylhydrazine, C-2 condenses directly and C-1 gets oxidized and condenses, giving the exact same osazone!  
• Exam Shortcut (Page 22). Any pair of C-2 epimeric aldoses (like D-Allose and D-Altrose, or D-Glucose and D-Mannose) along with their corresponding 2-ketose (like D-Fructose) will always form the identical osazone.
