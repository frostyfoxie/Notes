# Carbohydrates: Reducing Properties, Stereochemistry & Reactions of Glucose

---

## Module 1: Reducing Sugars & Mild Oxidizing Tests

A **reducing sugar** is any carbohydrate capable of acting as a reducing agent because it possesses—or can generate in solution—a free aldehyde group ($-\text{CHO}$) or an $\alpha$-hydroxy ketone group. When treated with mild alkaline oxidizing agents, the sugar donates electrons (oxidizes to a carboxylic acid salt), while the metal ions in the reagent gain electrons (reduce).

### 1. Why Functional Groups Act as Reducing Groups

#### A. The Aldehyde Group ($-\text{CHO}$)
* **Weak $\text{C}-\text{H}$ Bond:** In an aldehyde, the carbonyl carbon is bonded to a highly electronegative oxygen atom ($\text{C}=\text{O}$) and a hydrogen atom. Oxygen pulls electron density strongly away from the carbonyl carbon, which polarizes and weakens the $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bond.
* **Facile Oxidation:** Because the $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bond breaks easily, mild oxidants ($\text{Ag}^+$ or $\text{Cu}^{2+}$) can oxidize the aldehyde cleanly into a stable carboxylate ion ($-\text{COO}^-$) under basic conditions.

#### B. The $\alpha$-Hydroxy Carbonyl Group (Ketoses)
* **Why Ordinary Ketones Don't React:** Simple ketones lack a hydrogen atom attached to the carbonyl carbon, so they resist mild oxidation.
* **Base-Catalyzed Enediol Rearrangement:** An $\alpha$-hydroxy ketone has an $-\text{OH}$ group attached to the carbon immediately adjacent ($\alpha$-carbon) to the $\text{C}=\text{O}$ group. Because both Tollens' and Fehling's reagents are **alkaline (basic)**, the base catalyzes **keto–enol tautomerism** through a flat **enediol intermediate** ($\text{C}=\text{C}$ with $-\text{OH}$ on both carbons). This shifts the carbonyl group from C-2 to C-1, converting the ketose (e.g., fructose) into an oxidizable aldose (glucose/mannose).

---

### 2. Hemiacetals vs. Acetals (Why Rings Open or Stay Locked)

* **Hemiacetal Structure:** Formed when one molecule of an alcohol ($-\text{OH}$) reacts with an aldehyde ($-\text{CHO}$). The central carbon is bonded to **four different groups**:
  1. An alcohol group ($-\text{OH}$)
  2. An ether group ($-\text{OR}$)
  3. A carbon chain ($-\text{R}$)
  4. A hydrogen atom ($-\text{H}$)
* **Intramolecular Ring Formation in Sugars:** In aqueous solution, open-chain D-glucose cyclizes when the C-5 $-\text{OH}$ group attacks the electrophilic C-1 $-\text{CHO}$ carbon. C-1 becomes a **cyclic hemiacetal carbon** (known as the **anomeric carbon**).
* **Dynamic Equilibrium (Reversibility):** Hemiacetals are reversible in water. The ring continuously opens and closes in solution, briefly exposing the free $-\text{CHO}$ group so it can react with oxidizing reagents.
* **Acetal Structure (Glycosidic Bonds):** If a hemiacetal reacts with a *second* alcohol molecule, the $-\text{OH}$ group is replaced by another $-\text{OR}$ group, forming an **acetal** (bonded to two $-\text{OR}$ groups). Acetals are **locked** in neutral and basic solutions—they cannot open back up to expose an aldehyde, which makes sugars with only acetal linkages **non-reducing**.

### 3. Tollens' Reagent & Fehling's Solution

#### A. Tollens' Reagent (Ammoniacal Silver Nitrate)
* **Preparation:** Aqueous ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) is added dropwise to silver nitrate ($\text{AgNO}_3$) until the initial brown silver oxide ($\text{Ag}_2\text{O}$) precipitate dissolves, forming the active **diamminesilver(I) complex ion**: $[\text{Ag}(\text{NH}_3)_2]^+$.
* **Reaction & Observation:** $\text{Ag}^+$ is reduced to elemental metallic silver ($\text{Ag}^0$). On the clean glass wall of a test tube, this deposits as a characteristic **shiny silver mirror**.
  $$\text{R}-\text{CHO} + 2[\text{Ag}(\text{NH}_3)_2]^+ + 3\text{OH}^- \longrightarrow \text{R}-\text{COO}^- + 2\text{Ag}\downarrow + 4\text{NH}_3 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

#### B. Fehling's Solution
* **Preparation:** Prepared immediately before use by mixing equal volumes of two separate solutions:
  * **Fehling's A:** Aqueous blue solution of copper(II) sulfate ($\text{CuSO}_4$).
  * **Fehling's B:** Clear alkaline solution of **potassium sodium tartrate (Rochelle salt)** in sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$). *(Note: Tartrate ions complex with $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ to prevent it from precipitating as $\text{Cu(OH)}_2$ in the strong base.)*
* **Reaction & Observation:** Upon heating, deep blue copper(II) ions ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$) are reduced to copper(I) ions ($\text{Cu}^+$), forming an insoluble **brick-red precipitate** of copper(I) oxide ($\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$).
  $$\text{R}-\text{CHO} + 2\text{Cu}^{2+} + 5\text{OH}^- \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{R}-\text{COO}^- + \text{Cu}_2\text{O}\downarrow (\text{brick-red}) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

---

### 4. Comparison of Reducing vs. Non-Reducing Carbohydrates

| Carbohydrate Type | Examples | Tollens' / Fehling's Result | Chemical Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Monosaccharides** | Glucose, Fructose, Galactose | **Positive** | Rings open freely in aqueous solution to expose a free $-\text{CHO}$ group (directly for aldoses, or via base-catalyzed enediol tautomerism for ketoses). |
| **Reducing Disaccharides** | Maltose, Lactose | **Positive** | One of the two sugar rings retains a free **hemiacetal carbon** (anomeric carbon with an $-\text{OH}$), allowing that ring to open and form an aldehyde. |
| **Non-Reducing Disaccharides** | Sucrose | **Negative** | The glycosidic bond connects **both anomeric carbons** (C-1 of glucose and C-2 of fructose), locking both into acetal/ketal linkages that cannot open. |
| **Polysaccharides** | Starch, Cellulose, Glycogen | **Negative** | Almost all anomeric carbons are tied up in glycosidic bonds; the single free reducing end per massive polymer chain is negligible and too dilute to give a visible test. |

## Module 2: Stereochemistry of Carbohydrates

### 1. D and L Configuration (Fischer Projections)

When drawing open-chain carbohydrates in a vertical **Fischer projection**, the most oxidized carbon (such as $-\text{CHO}$ or $\text{C}=\text{O}$) is placed at the top (C-1).

* **How to Assign D or L:** Locate the **chiral carbon farthest from the carbonyl group** (the bottom-most chiral center; in hexoses, this is **C-5**).
  * **D-Configuration:** The $-\text{OH}$ group on this carbon points to the **Right**.
  * **L-Configuration:** The $-\text{OH}$ group on this carbon points to the **Left**.
* **Critical Exam Trap (D $\rightarrow$ L Conversion):** Converting a D-sugar (e.g., D-Galactose) into its L-isomer (L-Galactose) **does not** mean flipping only the bottom C-5 $-\text{OH}$ group. An L-sugar is the **complete mirror image (enantiomer)** of the D-sugar—you must invert the configuration of **every single chiral carbon** (Right $\leftrightarrow$ Left).
* **No Relation to Optical Rotation ($+ / -$ or $d / l$):** Capital **D** and **L** strictly indicate relative 3D configuration compared to D- and L-glyceraldehyde. They **do not** predict the direction of rotation of plane-polarized light.
  * *Example:* **D-Glucose** is dextrorotatory ($+$ / $d$), whereas **D-Fructose** is strongly levorotatory ($-$ / $l$, $-92^\circ$).

---

### 2. Stereoisomers of Aldohexoses

* An aldohexose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) has **4 chiral centers** (C-2, C-3, C-4, and C-5).
* Using the formula $2^n$ (where $n = 4$):
  $$\text{Total Stereoisomers} = 2^4 = 16$$
* **Distribution:**
  * **8 D-Aldohexoses:** D-Allose, D-Altrose, D-Glucose, D-Mannose, D-Gulose, D-Idose, D-Galactose, and D-Talose.
  * **8 L-Aldohexoses:** The exact mirror images of the 8 D-sugars.
* **Key Insight:** "Aldohexose" is the family name for all 16 isomers; **D-Glucose** is just one specific stereoisomer within this set.

#### Essential $-\text{OH}$ Orientation Patterns (Reading Top to Bottom: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5)
* **D-Allose:** Right, Right, Right, Right
* **D-Altrose:** Left, Right, Right, Right
* **D-Glucose:** **Right, Left, Right, Right** *(Core reference structure to memorize)*
* **D-Mannose:** Left, Left, Right, Right
* **D-Galactose:** Right, Left, Left, Right
* **D-Fructose (a 2-Ketohexose):** Has $-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$ at C-1 and $\text{C}=\text{O}$ at C-2. Its three chiral centers (**C-3, C-4, C-5**) are **Left, Right, Right**—identical to C-3, C-4, and C-5 of D-Glucose.

---

### 3. Enantiomers, Diastereomers, and Epimers

* **Enantiomers (Exact Mirror Images):** Stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other (every chiral center is inverted).
  * *Example:* D-Glucose and L-Glucose.
* **Diastereomers (Non-Mirror Stereoisomers):** Stereoisomers with the same molecular formula and connectivity that are **not** mirror images of each other (some chiral centers match, while one or more differ).
  * *Example:* D-Glucose and D-Galactose; D-Glucose and D-Allose.
* **Epimers (A Special Subset of Diastereomers):** Pairs of diastereomers that have the exact same configuration at every chiral carbon **except at one single chiral center**. *(Every epimer is a diastereomer, but not every diastereomer is an epimer.)*

#### Key Epimer Pairs for Exams
* **D-Glucose & D-Mannose:** **C-2 epimers** (differ only at C-2: Right in glucose, Left in mannose).
* **D-Glucose & D-Galactose:** **C-4 epimers** (differ only at C-4: Right in glucose, Left in galactose).
* **D-Allose & D-Glucose:** **C-3 epimers** (differ only at C-3: Right in allose, Left in glucose).
* **D-Allose & D-Altrose:** **C-2 epimers** (differ only at C-2: Right in allose, Left in altrose).

---

### 4. The Enediol Rearrangement (Base-Catalyzed Equilibration)

When **D-Fructose**, **D-Glucose**, or **D-Mannose** is placed in a mild alkaline (basic) solution, base-catalyzed tautomerization creates a planar **enediol intermediate** across C-1 and C-2:

$$\begin{array}{ccccccc}
\text{CHO} & & \text{CH}-\text{OH} & & \text{CH}_2\text{OH} \\
| & & || & & | \\
\text{H}-\text{C}-\text{OH} & \xrightleftharpoons{\text{OH}^-} & \text{C}-\text{OH} & \xrightleftharpoons{\text{OH}^-} & \text{C}=\text{O} \\
| & & | & & | \\
\text{(C-3 to C-6)} & & \text{(C-3 to C-6)} & & \text{(C-3 to C-6)} \\
\text{\textbf{D-Glucose}} & & \text{\textbf{Enediol}} & & \text{\textbf{D-Fructose}}
\end{array}$$

Because the C-2 carbon in the enediol is flat ($sp^2$), protonation during tautomerization back to the aldehyde can occur from either face, yielding both **D-Glucose** (C-2 $-\text{OH}$ Right) and **D-Mannose** (C-2 $-\text{OH}$ Left). Consequently, all three sugars exist in equilibrium in alkaline solution—explaining why D-Fructose gives positive Tollens' and Fehling's tests.

## Module 3: Structural Elucidation & Chemical Reactions of Glucose

### Basic Profile of Natural D-Glucose
* **Classification:** Monosaccharide (cannot be hydrolyzed into simpler sugars); Aldohexose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$).
* **Optical Activity:** Natural D-glucose is dextrorotatory ($d$ or $+$).

---

### Reaction 1: Prolonged Heating with Hydriodic Acid ($\text{HI} / \text{Red P}, \Delta$)
* **Reagent:** $\text{HI}$ in the presence of red phosphorus with prolonged heating.
* **Action:** An exhaustive, powerful reducing system that strips away all oxygen functional groups ($-\text{CHO}$ and $-\text{OH}$) and replaces them with hydrogen atoms.
* **Product:** **n-Hexane** ($\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_3$).
* **Structural Proof:** Forming *normal* (unbranched) hexane proves that all **6 carbon atoms in glucose are linked in a straight, unbranched chain**.

---

### Reaction 2: Reaction with Hydroxylamine ($\text{NH}_2\text{OH}$) or $\text{HCN}$
* **Reagents:** Hydroxylamine ($\text{NH}_2\text{OH}$) or hydrogen cyanide ($\text{HCN}$).
* **Action:**
  * With $\text{NH}_2\text{OH}$, nucleophilic addition-elimination occurs at the carbonyl carbon, losing water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) to form a $\text{C}=\text{N}-\text{OH}$ bond (**Glucoxime**).
  * With $\text{HCN}$, nucleophilic addition forms a **glucose cyanohydrin** ($\text{R}-\text{CH(OH)CN}$).
* **Structural Proof:** Because $\text{NH}_2\text{OH}$ and $\text{HCN}$ are characteristic reagents for aldehydes and ketones, this proves the presence of a **carbonyl group ($\text{C}=\text{O}$)** in glucose. *(Note: It does not distinguish between an aldehyde and a ketone.)*

---

### Reaction 3: Acetylation with Excess Acetic Anhydride ($\text{Ac}_2\text{O} / \text{Pyridine}$)
* **Reagent:** Excess acetic anhydride ($\text{Ac}_2\text{O}$, where $\text{Ac} = -\text{COCH}_3$) in pyridine.
* **Action:** Converts every hydroxyl group ($-\text{OH}$) into an acetate ester group ($-\text{OAc}$). The top $-\text{CHO}$ group remains untouched.
* **Product:** **Glucose pentaacetate**.
* **Structural Proof:**
  1. Forming a *penta*-acetate proves there are **five $-\text{OH}$ groups**.
  2. Because glucose pentaacetate is a stable compound, all five $-\text{OH}$ groups must be attached to **five different carbon atoms** (two $-\text{OH}$ groups on the same carbon would form an unstable geminal diol and lose water).

---

### Reaction 4: Mild Oxidation with Bromine Water ($\text{Br}_2 / \text{H}_2\text{O}$) or Tollens' Reagent
* **Reagents:** Bromine water ($\text{Br}_2 / \text{H}_2\text{O}$) or Tollens' reagent followed by acidification ($\text{H}^+$).
* **Action:** Mild oxidants selectively oxidize the aldehyde group ($-\text{CHO}$) at C-1 into a carboxylic acid ($-\text{COOH}$) without breaking carbon–carbon bonds or oxidizing the five alcohol groups.
* **Product:** **D-Gluconic acid** ($\text{HOOC}-(\text{CHOH})_4-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$).
* **Structural Proof:** Proves that the carbonyl group ($\text{C}=\text{O}$) is specifically an **aldehyde group ($-\text{CHO}$)** located at the end of the chain (C-1).
* **Exam Distinguishing Test:** **Bromine water ($\text{Br}_2 / \text{H}_2\text{O}$)** is neutral/slightly acidic, so ketoses cannot undergo base-catalyzed enediol isomerization. Therefore, **D-Glucose reacts with $\text{Br}_2 / \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (decolorizing it to form gluconic acid), whereas D-Fructose does not react at all.**

---

### Reaction 5: Strong Oxidation with Concentrated Nitric Acid ($\text{HNO}_3$)
* **Reagent:** Concentrated nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$), starting from either D-glucose or D-gluconic acid.
* **Action:** Nitric acid is strong enough to oxidize both terminal groups:
  * Top aldehyde ($-\text{CHO}$ at C-1) $\longrightarrow$ $-\text{COOH}$
  * Bottom primary ($1^\circ$) alcohol ($-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$ at C-6) $\longrightarrow$ $-\text{COOH}$
* **Why Middle Carbons Stay Intact:** Carbons C-2, C-3, C-4, and C-5 carry secondary ($2^\circ$) alcohol groups. Oxidizing $2^\circ$ alcohols requires much harsher conditions that cleave the $\text{C}-\text{C}$ skeleton; under $\text{HNO}_3$, the unhindered $1^\circ$ alcohol at C-6 is oxidized selectively.
* **Product:** **D-Glucaric acid** (also called **D-saccharic acid**), a 6-carbon dicarboxylic acid.
* **Structural Proof:** Converting D-gluconic acid (1 $-\text{COOH}$) into D-glucaric acid (2 $-\text{COOH}$) proves that one of the five $-\text{OH}$ groups is a **primary ($1^\circ$) alcohol ($-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$)** situated at the terminal C-6 position.

---

### Reaction 6: Mild Reduction with $\text{NaBH}_4$ or $\text{H}_2 / \text{Ni}$
* **Reagents:** Sodium borohydride in methanol ($\text{NaBH}_4 / \text{MeOH}$) or catalytic hydrogenation ($\text{H}_2 / \text{Ni}$).
* **Action on D-Glucose:** Mild reduction selectively adds $\text{H}_2$ across the C-1 $\text{C}=\text{O}$ bond, converting $-\text{CHO}$ into $-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$ while leaving C-2 through C-6 untouched.
  * **Product:** Only **D-Sorbitol** (glucitol). Because C-1 becomes $-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$ (achiral), no new stereocenter is formed.
* **Comparison Trap — Reduction of D-Fructose:**
  * In D-Fructose, the $\text{C}=\text{O}$ group is at **C-2**, which is a flat $sp^2$ carbon.
  * Upon reduction with $\text{NaBH}_4$ or $\text{H}_2 / \text{Ni}$, C-2 becomes a tetrahedral $sp^3$ **chiral center** ($-\text{CH(OH)}-$).
  * Because hydride ($\text{H}^-$) can attack the flat C-2 carbonyl equally well from either face (Right or Left), **D-Fructose yields an epimeric mixture of two products**:
    1. **D-Sorbitol** (new $-\text{OH}$ on the Right; identical to reduced D-glucose)
    2. **D-Mannitol** (new $-\text{OH}$ on the Left; identical to reduced D-mannose)

---

### Reaction 7: Osazone Formation with Excess Phenylhydrazine ($\text{PhNHNH}_2$)
* **Reagent:** **3 equivalents (excess)** of phenylhydrazine ($\text{Ph}-\text{NH}-\text{NH}_2$) with heat ($\Delta$).
* **Overall Result:** Both C-1 and C-2 react to form $\text{C}=\text{N}-\text{NHPh}$ bonds, precipitating a bright yellow crystalline solid called **glucosazone** (an osazone).

#### Step-by-Step Role of the 3 Equivalents of $\text{PhNHNH}_2$
Normally, aldehydes react with only 1 equivalent of phenylhydrazine. Because glucose is an $\alpha$-hydroxy aldehyde (having an $-\text{OH}$ at C-2), the reaction proceeds through three stages upon heating:
1. **1st Equivalent (Hydrazone Formation):** $\text{PhNHNH}_2$ condenses with the C-1 aldehyde ($-\text{CHO}$) to form **glucose phenylhydrazone** ($-\text{CH}=\text{N}-\text{NHPh}$), releasing **1 mole of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$**. *(If kept cold, the reaction stops here.)*
2. **Oxidation of C-2 (Internal Tautomerization / Redox):** Upon heating, the hydrazone tautomerizes and undergoes elimination/oxidation of the C-2 secondary alcohol ($-\text{CHOH}-$) into a ketone ($\text{C}=\text{O}$), while C-1 becomes an imine ($-\text{CH}=\text{NH}$), releasing **1 mole of aniline ($\text{PhNH}_2$)**.
3. **2nd & 3rd Equivalents (Completing the Osazone):** The remaining two molecules of $\text{PhNHNH}_2$ condense with the newly formed C-1 imine (releasing **1 mole of $\text{NH}_3$**) and the C-2 ketone (releasing **1 mole of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$**), locking in the bis-hydrazone (**osazone**).

#### Stoichiometry & Byproducts (High-Yield MCQ Fact)
$$\text{1 D-Glucose} + \mathbf{3\text{ PhNHNH}_2} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{1 Glucosazone} + \mathbf{1\text{ PhNH}_2} + \mathbf{1\text{ NH}_3} + \mathbf{2\text{ H}_2\text{O}}$$

#### Why the Reaction Stops at C-2 (Does Not Continue to C-3, C-4, C-5)
Once both C-1 and C-2 bear $=\text{N}-\text{NHPh}$ groups, the molecule folds into a highly stable **6-membered ring stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonding (chelation)** between the C-1 and C-2 hydrazone groups. This chelate stability halts any further oxidation down the carbon chain.

#### Why D-Glucose, D-Mannose, and D-Fructose Form the Exact Same Osazone
* During osazone formation, **C-2** changes from a tetrahedral $sp^3$ chiral center into a flat $sp^2$ carbon ($\text{C}=\text{N}-\text{NHPh}$). Thus, **the chirality at C-2 is destroyed**, while **C-3, C-4, and C-5 remain completely untouched**.
* **D-Glucose** and **D-Mannose** are C-2 epimers (identical at C-3, C-4, C-5), and **D-Fructose** has a $\text{C}=\text{O}$ at C-2 with the exact same configuration at C-3, C-4, and C-5.
* **Rule:** Any pair of C-2 epimeric aldoses and their corresponding 2-ketose always produce the **identical osazone**.
